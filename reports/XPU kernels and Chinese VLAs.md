# Pick π0.5, then compile it down for Intel

The best VLA to run on an SO-101 with Intel-only hardware is still **π0.5**, not a Chinese model. Fine-tune it with Physical AI Studio's SO-101 SnapFlow recipe, which distils it to one denoising step, and deploy it through Intel's OpenVINO π0.5 pipeline with INT8 weights and Real-Time Chunking. If the training GPU has less than 24 GB, or a one-hour smoke test shows π0.5 cannot finish training in time, fall back to **SmolVLA**. No open Chinese VLA from 2025–2026 has independent evidence of beating π0.5. Each lab beats it on a benchmark it chose, and on the independent RoboDojo real-robot benchmark **π0.5 led at 12.8% success versus 7.2% for the best Chinese entry (InternVLA-A1)** ([RoboDojo, arXiv 2607.04434](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.04434v1)). Several Chinese models clearly beat SmolVLA, though. The most practical on Intel are **X-VLA (0.9B)** and **EVO1 (0.77B)**, both native in LeRobot, but neither has an Intel or OpenVINO deployment path, so each is a research gamble and not a demo-day plan. On the optimisation side, Intel's own work gives the measured path. Its π0.5 pipeline on a Panther Lake iGPU went from **555 ms to 172 ms per 50-action chunk** using OpenVINO IR, INT8 weight-only compression, a single batched vision call and built-in GPU-plugin fusions ([Intel π0.5 paper](https://docs.openedgeplatform.intel.com/shared_media/publication-optimize-pi0.5-paper.pdf)). At 30 Hz a 50-action chunk lasts about 1.67 s, so that latency already fits. Hand-written kernels are the last lever, not the first. For ACT the playbook's plan stands, and the main additions are static-shape OpenVINO settings, plus graph capture if it runs in PyTorch.

## No open Chinese VLA independently beats π0.5, but several beat SmolVLA

Almost every Chinese lab released a VLA in 2025–2026 that claims to beat π0.5, and almost every claim rests on a saturated or self-chosen benchmark. **LIBERO no longer separates models**: X-VLA reports 98.1%, Xiaomi-Robotics-0 98.7% and Galaxea G0.5 98.9% ([X-VLA](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.10274v1); [Xiaomi-R0](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.12684); [G0.5](https://www.sourcepulse.org/projects/31538735)), all within protocol noise of each other. The RoboTwin 2.0 margins are real but come from the authors' own runs: InternVLA-A1-3B scores 89.40/89.64 against π0.5's 86.76/86.96 ([HF card](https://huggingface.co/InternRobotics/InternVLA-A1-3B)), and LingBot-VLA claims +5.8 to +9.9 points with depth ([arXiv 2601.18692](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.18692)).

The two third-party benchmarks disagree, and the disagreement matters. On **RoboChallenge Table30**, a remotely run real-robot benchmark, **DM0 (2B, Dexmal/StepFun) reached 62.0% specialist success and Spirit v1.5 51.0%, against π0.5's 42.67%** ([Emergent Mind Table30](https://www.emergentmind.com/topics/table30); [CGTN](https://news.cgtn.com/news/2026-01-14/Chinese-AI-startup-tops-global-embodied-intelligence-benchmark-1JV32RBb7QQ/p.html)). RoboChallenge's organisers appear to be linked to Dexmal, which should temper DM0's lead, though that link is unverified. On **RoboDojo** (HKU MMLab and collaborators, July 2026), the same DM0 **scored 0.0% on real robots**, while π0.5 led and the Chinese models trailed: InternVLA-A1 7.2%, Galaxea G0 4.4%, Xiaomi-R0 3.9%, X-VLA 3.3% ([RoboDojo](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.04434v1)). The most likely explanation is that Table30 and the self-reported numbers reward per-task fine-tuning on familiar embodiments, while RoboDojo tests harder long-horizon generalisation. A government press release says DM0.5 later "topped the RoboDojo rankings" ([Liangjiang gov](https://english.liangjiang.gov.cn/2026-08/28/c_1209436.htm)). That claim is unverified and is not in the paper.

Against SmolVLA the picture flips. In RoboDojo simulation, **X-VLA scored 6.52% against SmolVLA's 0.85%**, nearly matching π0.5's 6.91% ([RoboDojo](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.04434v1)). The only SO-101-specific comparison, from Hiroshima University, reportedly puts **WALL-X at 51.25% against π0.5 56.25%, ACT 33.75% and SmolVLA 32.5%** over four real SO-101 tasks ([HF papers 2606.08881](https://huggingface.co/papers/2606.08881); [blog summary](https://mcobzarenco-fontaine-blog.static.hf.space/papers/so101-vla-benchmark.html)). Those figures come from a blog summary and were not checked against the paper. Taken together, the evidence gives a clear ordering for this hackathon: π0.5 first, a group of Chinese models (WALL-OSS, X-VLA, InternVLA-A1) in between, and SmolVLA roughly level with ACT. That last point matters. **SmolVLA buys language conditioning, not success rate, over the playbook's ACT.**

## Only three Chinese VLAs are realistic on an Intel-only SO-101

Running on Intel narrows the field sharply, because three conditions must hold at once: the model is in LeRobot (or there is no time to port it), it fits a 16–24 GB Intel card for fine-tuning, and it has no hard CUDA dependency. LeRobot `main` now ships native policies for `xvla`, `evo1`, `eo1`, `wall_oss_05`/`wall_x`, `lingbot_vla_v2`, `groot` and (probably) Galaxea `g05`. InternVLA, RDT2, GO-1, Being-H0 and VLA-Adapter are absent ([LeRobot policies dir](https://github.com/huggingface/lerobot/tree/main/src/lerobot/policies)). Intel's Physical AI Studio, which owns the XPU-training-to-OpenVINO-export path the playbook relies on, supports **only ACT, π0.5, SmolVLA, MolmoAct2, RLDX-1, XR0 and Cosmos3**. None of them is Chinese ([physical-ai-studio](https://github.com/open-edge-platform/physical-ai-studio)).

X-VLA is the most interesting Chinese option. However, **nobody has published X-VLA, EVO1 or any other Chinese VLA running on Intel**, and getting one into OpenVINO means writing your own `torch.export`/`ov.convert_model` split. For any out-of-tree repo, the standard XPU workaround is `attn_implementation="sdpa"`, removing `flash-attn`/`xformers`/`bitsandbytes`, and replacing `.cuda()` and `torch.cuda.amp`. Budget a day for that, not an hour.

### The recommendation depends on training memory more than on accuracy

Fine-tuning, not inference, is the binding constraint. LeRobot's guide puts π0/π0.5/X-VLA/WALL-X at **24–40 GB peak, "24 GB tight at batch 1"**. On 4×H100, 5k steps took 3 h 41 min for π0.5 against 28 min for SmolVLA ([LeRobot hardware guide](https://huggingface.co/docs/lerobot/main/hardware_guide)). There are no published Arc training throughputs for any VLA, so the team has to measure its own. The decision rule is therefore hardware-driven. With a **24 GB Arc Pro B60 (or a Panther Lake/Lunar Lake machine with ≥32 GB shared RAM)**, train π0.5 through `configs/physicalai/pi05/so101/snapflow.yaml` with gradient checkpointing, batch 1–2, bf16 and a frozen vision encoder. Time the first 200 steps, and abandon it within the first hour if the projected run exceeds the budget. With a **16 GB A770/B580**, π0.5 does not fit, so train SmolVLA, with `freeze_vision_encoder=True` if memory is tight. In both cases ACT remains the guaranteed baseline, as the playbook already specifies. The VLA is a parallel track that earns its place only if it beats ACT on held-out lighting.

## Intel's measured π0.5 work shows where the latency actually goes

The single most useful source is Intel's May 2026 paper, which profiles π0.5-DROID (3 cameras at 224², 64 language tokens, 10 denoising steps, 50-action chunk) on a Core Ultra X7 358H. **GEMMs are over 99% of operations, and the VLM accounts for 95% of compute against 4% for the action expert.** However, the action expert's small 50-token matmuls are **dispatch-bound at roughly 104 µs per unfused kernel launch** ([Intel π0.5 paper](https://docs.openedgeplatform.intel.com/shared_media/publication-optimize-pi0.5-paper.pdf)). That split explains the optimisation order. Shrinking and compressing the VLM prefix wins the most milliseconds. Fusing and graph-capturing the denoising loop wins the rest. Distilling to fewer steps removes most of the action-expert term outright.

The measured wins stack as follows. The OpenVINO FP16 IR with static shapes is the base. **INT8 weight-only compression via NNCF halved model size and gave more than 30% speedup with no calibration data.** Batching all cameras into one SigLIP call removes 50–67% of redundant weight traffic. The GPU plugin's SDPA fusion and `FuseGatedMLP` (gate/up/Swish/mul/down collapsed into one oneDNN primitive) attack the launch-bound expert. Together these reached **172 ms per chunk at 90% LIBERO success** ([Intel π0.5 paper](https://docs.openedgeplatform.intel.com/shared_media/publication-optimize-pi0.5-paper.pdf)). The paper's PyTorch baselines are internally inconsistent: 555 ms eager in §4 and 294 ms with PyTorch 2.12 at 40 W in §6 ([OEP article](https://docs.openedgeplatform.intel.com/dev/OEP-articles/publications/optimizing-pi0.5-lva-model.html)). So OpenVINO's real advantage over current PyTorch-XPU is somewhere between 1.7× and 3.2×. All of this is vendor-reported, and no one has reproduced it independently.

The heterogeneous split is Intel's showcase, but it matters less for this team. Intel runs the vision encoder and LLM on the iGPU and the action expert on the NPU, sharing the per-layer KV cache zero-copy as USM-host tensors. Under RTC that turns the refill bottleneck from T_VE+T_LE+T_AE into roughly max(T_VE+T_LE, T_AE) ([Intel π0.5 paper](https://docs.openedgeplatform.intel.com/shared_media/publication-optimize-pi0.5-paper.pdf)). The two devices share one memory controller, and no millisecond figures were published for the split. The split exists only on Lunar Lake and Panther Lake. On an Arc discrete card the KV handoff would cross PCIe, so the split does not pay there. Its value is overlap and power, not single-shot latency.

Model-level cuts dominate kernel-level ones on a 153 GB/s iGPU. Even a fully optimised π0 on an RTX 4090 needs **20.0 / 27.3 / 36.8 ms for 1 / 2 / 3 camera views**, against a roofline of about 20 ms ([Running VLAs at Real-time Speed, arXiv 2510.26742](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.26742v1)). The iGPU has roughly one sixth of that bandwidth, so kernel tuning alone cannot reach 30 Hz single-shot inference. Three hardware-agnostic levers stack on top. **SnapFlow distils π0.5 to one denoising step at 98.75% LIBERO against 97.75% for the 10-step teacher** ([SnapFlow](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.05656)). Our rig uses three views (`wrist`, `top` and the side `inspect` camera). That is about 37 ms for 3 views versus 27 ms for 2 on the 4090, so if latency is tight, test dropping `inspect` from the policy input, since its main job is defect detection. Avoid adding depth as a further image. VLA-aware low-bit weights also help, with a caveat. **π0.5 tolerates W4A8 (97.6% LIBERO, memory 4.27 → 1.28 GB)** ([QuantVLA](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.20309)), but generic GPTQ/AWQ applied to the full stack collapsed it to 11–16% ([HoloQ-VLA](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.28803)). INT8 weight-only is therefore the safe level, and INT4 must leave the action expert alone. Real-Time Chunking then hides whatever latency remains. Inference-time RTC adds a vector-Jacobian product through the expert at each step, while training-time RTC avoids that overhead ([arXiv 2512.05964](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.05964)). That favours the training-time variant on compute-starved iGPUs.

## Ordered optimisation checklist for π0.5 (or SmolVLA) on Intel

The checklist is ordered by expected gain per hour of effort. Steps 1–7 are the main OpenVINO path and should all fit inside a hackathon. Steps 8–10 are conditional. The PyTorch-XPU column is the fallback if OpenVINO export breaks, which the playbook already lists as a risk.

Two caveats on step 6. First, OpenVINO's activation dynamic quantization is **skipped at 64 tokens or fewer** ([OpenVINO blog](https://blog.openvino.ai/blog-posts/dynamic-quantization-support-from-gpu-with-xmx)). The VLM prefix (hundreds of tokens) therefore gets INT8 XMX compute, but the 50-token expert runs as weight-dequant GEMMs, which is one more reason its cost is about bandwidth and dispatch. Second, custom OpenCL kernels through the `CustomLayer` XML path are opaque to the plugin's fusion passes and do not use oneDNN/XMX ([custom GPU ops docs](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/openvinotoolkit/openvino/master/docs/articles_en/documentation/openvino-extensibility/custom-gpu-operations.rst)). Intel's wins came from matching the built-in patterns, not from writing kernels.

### The PyTorch-XPU fallback path has one trap

If export fails or the team wants to iterate in Python, a similar stack exists in PyTorch. Run in bf16 (or fp16 on Arc A-series, which is often faster there) so matmuls hit XMX. Then use `torch.compile(..., mode="max-autotune-no-cudagraphs", dynamic=False)`. Then capture the whole fixed-step denoising loop with `torch.xpu.graph` around static buffers. The trap is that **`mode="reduce-overhead"` is a silent no-op on XPU as of PyTorch 2.15**: Inductor gates cudagraphs to CUDA, and the fix was still a draft PR ([torch-xpu-ops #5338](https://github.com/intel/torch-xpu-ops/issues/5338)). Manual `torch.xpu.XPUGraph` capture therefore has to be done explicitly. On an **Arc A770 it must be forced** with `SYCL_UR_USE_LEVEL_ZERO_V2=1 NEOReadDebugKeys=1 OverrideRecordReplayGraphCapability=1`, which gave 111 µs/step against ~175 µs eager on a 64-step loop ([arc-a770-pytorch-notes](https://github.com/BarzinL/arc-a770-pytorch-notes)). That override is not validated by Intel. Without graph capture, enabling Level Zero direct submission (`NEOReadDebugKeys=1 EnableDirectSubmission=1`) cut A770 host launch cost from ~14 µs to ~3 µs and gave 1.5× on llama.cpp SYCL decode (same source). For memory, torchao `Int4WeightOnlyConfig(group_size=128, int4_packing_format="plain_int32")` on the VLM linears gave 1.56–2.14× decode speedup for 3–4B LLMs on a B580 ([PyTorch 2.8+TorchAO blog](https://pytorch.org/blog/pytorch-2-8torchao-unlock-efficient-llm-inference-on-intel-ai-pcs/)). Call `unwrap_tensor_subclass_parameters` before compiling.

Attention needs one hardware-specific check. The SYCL-TLA FlashAttention kernels are AOT-built only for PVC and Battlemage ([pytorch#201409](https://github.com/pytorch/pytorch/pull/201409)), so Lunar Lake, Panther Lake and Arc A-series fall back to oneDNN SDPA. π0.5's Gemma backbone uses **head_dim 256**, which may fall off the flash path even on Battlemage, because sources conflict on whether 256 is supported. Time `sdpa_kernel([SDPBackend.FLASH_ATTENTION])` against `[SDPBackend.OVERRIDEABLE]` once on the real card rather than assuming either one. Custom Triton kernels are worth writing only for elementwise chains such as the flow-matching Euler update. For those, use `tl.make_tensor_descriptor`, because pointer-arithmetic kernels run 2× to 30× slower on XPU and block pointers were removed in Triton-XPU v3.9 ([Triton-XPU README](https://github.com/intel/intel-xpu-backend-for-triton)). Large GEMMs belong to oneDNN, which beats Triton bf16 bmm by about 14% ([#8723](https://github.com/intel/intel-xpu-backend-for-triton/issues/8723)).

### What changes for ACT

The playbook's ACT plan is correct, and this research adds four refinements. First, **quantising ACT is not worth it**: at about 80M parameters it is launch-bound rather than bandwidth-bound, so INT8 gains little and dequant overhead can hurt. Keep it FP16 in OpenVINO. Second, apply checklist steps 0, 3, 6 and 7 (pinned stack, static IR, verified fusions, LATENCY config with `CACHE_DIR`) and benchmark median/p99 with `InferenceLatencyBenchmark`. Third, if ACT runs in PyTorch, use bf16 + `torch.compile` + a manual `torch.xpu.graph` capture of the single forward pass, with direct submission as the cheap fallback. The playbook's line "`torch.compile` is supported on XPU" is true but incomplete, because the overhead-reduction mode silently does nothing. Fourth, the playbook statement that "no published OpenVINO latencies exist for π0.5" is now out of date: Intel's 172 ms per chunk on Panther Lake is the reference to beat, although no Arc or Lunar Lake numbers exist for any policy.

## Conclusion

The question of whether to use a Chinese VLA has a fairly settled answer for now. The models that beat π0.5 do so on benchmarks their own labs chose, and the strongest independent real-robot test reverses most of those wins. The decisive factor on Intel is ecosystem, not model quality. π0.5 is the only VLA with a vendor-optimised, measured path from XPU training through OpenVINO, INT8, RTC and a GPU/NPU split, plus an SO-101 distillation recipe. Any Chinese model starts from zero on that path. X-VLA is the one worth watching: it is small, LeRobot-native, SO-101-aware, and nearly level with π0.5 in RoboDojo simulation. One person could produce useful information by smoke-testing it on `xpu` for 30 minutes, but it should not be the demo policy.

The optimisation lesson changes the playbook's framing too. On Intel hardware, "kernel-level" work for VLAs mostly means shaping the model so the compiler's existing fusions fire, and graph-capturing what is left. Hand-writing kernels is the last resort. The levers that move the needle most are architectural: one denoising step, keeping the camera count small (our three: `wrist`, `top`, `inspect`), and INT8 weights. That makes the VLA track compatible with a hackathon timeline, provided the team times the first 200 training steps and falls back to SmolVLA, or simply keeps ACT, without hesitation.
